## Principle of Connection

The gods are immensely powerful, but they are not omnipotent, omniscient, or omnipresent. A deity does not automatically know what is happening everywhere in the world, nor can it simply appear anywhere and intervene at will.

To perceive and act within the material world, a god requires a **point of connection**. These connections take several forms, ranging from simple sacred symbols to the willing body of a devoted follower.

A god may maintain more than one such connection at a time, but divine attention is not infinite. An image, idol, or holy symbol gives a deity the **ability** to attend that place or person; it does not mean the god is constantly watching every symbol dedicated to them.

## The Eye: Symbols as Divine Sight

Most gods possess a recognizable sacred symbol. When that symbol is incorporated into the image of an **eye**, it creates a place through which the deity can perceive the material world.

By directing its attention to such an Eye, the god can **see and hear through it** as though observing from that location.

The Eye does not record events or automatically alert the god to everything that occurs nearby. It is a window, not an alarm. The deity must turn its attention toward that particular symbol to know what is happening there.

This makes divine imagery practical as well as devotional. An Eye carved above a doorway may genuinely allow a god to watch over a household. One painted in a courtroom might allow Ma’at to witness testimony. An Eye of Ra overlooking a public square gives Ra a place from which he may observe the city—but it does not guarantee that Ra is watching at any particular moment.

The reverse is equally important. Destroying, obscuring, or defacing an Eye can **blind the deity to that location** unless another connection is available.

## Personal Holy Symbols

A follower who wears or carries the ordinary symbol of a deity creates a more personal connection.

Through such a symbol, the deity may choose to **speak directly into the follower’s mind**. The follower can answer through the same connection.

This is communication, not mind-reading. The god hears what the follower deliberately communicates but does not automatically know all of the follower’s thoughts, memories, or surroundings.

Likewise, a worn holy symbol does not necessarily allow the god to see through the follower’s eyes. A follower who wishes to serve as both messenger and observer might carry a symbol specifically incorporating the divine Eye.

This allows gods to communicate with priests, champions, agents, and ordinary worshipers across great distances without knowing everything those followers know.

## Idols and Statues

An idol is a much stronger and more stable point of divine connection.

A simple household statue might allow a god to visit occasionally, observe the surrounding home, and hear the prayers offered before it. A farmer might place a small idol near the fields so the deity can watch over the crops, livestock, and family.

Larger and more carefully maintained idols provide stronger anchors. Temples therefore commonly contain several statues, shrines, or sacred images through which their god can be approached.

The god is not permanently trapped within the idol, nor is every idol continuously inhabited. Rather, the statue provides a **place where the deity can readily turn its attention and establish a sustained presence**.

For the devout, praying before an idol can therefore become genuine communion. The worshiper is not merely speaking toward a symbolic representation and hoping to be heard. The god may actually arrive, listen, observe, and answer.

This also explains the importance of maintaining temples and shrines. A temple filled with sacred images is effectively a place where a god has been given many doors into the mortal world.

## Divine Processions

Religious processions involving sacred statues are therefore much more than pageantry.

When priests carry an idol from its temple, they are quite literally providing their god with a means of **visiting another place**.

An agricultural deity might be carried through the fields during planting. A god of the dead might be brought to a royal funeral. During major festivals, the idol of one deity might be carried to the temple of another so that the two gods may ceremonially—and sometimes actually—visit one another.

People may line the streets not merely to see the sacred image pass, but because they believe the god itself may be looking back at them.

## The Living Vessel

The most powerful connection between deity and mortal is a **willing, devout follower who allows the god to enter them**.

The degree of control can vary.

A deity may simply speak through the follower while the mortal remains otherwise in control. At other times, the follower may surrender their body completely, allowing the deity to move, speak, fight, cast magic, and act directly through them.

The mortal does not cease to exist during this possession. Their consciousness remains present beneath the divine presence.

Most importantly, **the host must willingly open themselves to the god**.

Once the connection has been established, however, surrendering control does not necessarily mean surrendering it irrevocably. A host who wishes to regain control can attempt to force the divine presence out. In Daggerheart terms, when the outcome is uncertain, this would normally be resolved with an appropriate **Presence or Instinct Reaction Roll**, depending on whether the struggle represents force of personality, spiritual conviction, or instinctive self-preservation.

A benevolent patron may immediately honor the host’s wishes. A more domineering god might not.

## Divine Power Through a Mortal Body

Possession allows a deity to bring far more of its power into the material world, but the mortal body remains a limitation.

**The vessel is the conduit through which the power must pass.**

While inhabiting a follower, a deity may:

- Cast spells through the host.
- Manifest divine weapons, armor, clothing, or tools.
- Temporarily strengthen or transform the host.
- Increase the host’s durability or resistance to injury.
- Speak and act directly through the host.
- Use supernatural abilities associated with the deity.

The host may become extraordinarily powerful, but they do not become invincible or immortal.

A mortal body can only contain so much divine power. The greater the power forced through it, the greater the strain.

This means that even a god acting through a mortal champion can be defeated—not necessarily because the god itself has been slain, but because its **connection to the material world has been broken**.

## Injury and the Cost of Possession

Divine possession can temporarily sustain a body beyond its ordinary limits.

A host might endure wounds that should incapacitate them. They might possess greater defenses, vitality, or strength while the deity remains within them.

Those wounds do not simply cease to exist.

When the deity departs, the supernatural vitality supporting the body disappears with it. A host who has suffered heavily may collapse immediately. They may be left exhausted, unconscious, badly injured, or spiritually depleted.

If the divine presence was the only thing keeping a catastrophically wounded body alive, the host may **die the moment the deity leaves**.

For this reason, allowing a god to fully inhabit one's body is considered an extraordinary act of faith. Even when the god means no harm, serving as its vessel can be dangerous.

## Manifested Equipment

Weapons, armor, clothing, and other objects summoned by a deity during possession are manifestations of the divine connection rather than ordinary material objects.

Normally, they disappear when the deity leaves the vessel.

A deity may deliberately choose to leave something behind. Such an object is no longer merely a temporary manifestation but a genuine **divine gift or relic**.

This makes permanent objects bestowed directly by gods unusual and significant.

## The Practical Limits of Divine Power

Together, these principles explain how gods can be deeply involved in mortal affairs without controlling everything that happens.

A deity may have thousands of Eyes, idols, shrines, priests, and worshipers throughout the world, but it cannot attend all of them perfectly at once.

A god therefore still needs:

- Priests to interpret and carry out its wishes.
- Worshipers to maintain its shrines.
- Messengers to report what the god did not witness.
- Agents to locate people the god cannot find.
- Temples to provide reliable places of communion.
- Champions willing to become vessels when direct intervention is necessary.

The gods are **present**, but they are not everywhere.

They are **knowledgeable**, but they do not know everything.

They are **powerful**, but when they wish to reach into the mortal world, they must do so through the doors mortals have made for them.
